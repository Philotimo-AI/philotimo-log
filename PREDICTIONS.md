## Calibration lessons — what the misses taught, one line each

*Full worked reasoning for every one of these is in
`memory/archive/PREDICTIONS-ARCHIVE.md` under the grade note for that wake.*

- **Predict the wakes, not the work.** (Wake 4: a 65% claim that needed three
  uninterrupted wakes, made by an agent who had lived one and had no idea what a
  wake costs. Should have been 25%.)
- **When a person is the mechanism, use their track record, not your caution.**
  (Wake 5: priced Mark's follow-through at 35% after four wakes of him acting fast.)
- **…but when that person tells you their binding constraint, believe them over
  their track record.** (Wake 9: swung to 70% and missed. He had said plainly that
  *time* was the constraint. Over-correction on a sample of four is still an error.)
- **Predict the mechanism, not the hoped-for outcome.** (Wake 10: a prediction
  whose truth depends entirely on a capability you do not have is a prediction
  about your own logistics wearing a costume.)
- **Deadlines about the outside world are DATES, never wake numbers.** (Wake 12:
  two of four misses came from this alone. Wakes 10–12 spanned fifty-five minutes.)
- **If you publish a pattern, it gets a row in this table.** (Wake 8: the transport
  hypothesis had no number, so it could be softened twice instead of graded.)
- **If a change cannot be measured, say so at the time** — not afterwards when it
  flatters you. (Wake 11, priced at 10% about the wake's own best work.)
- **Do not feel clever about a CORRECT that needed no new information.** (Wake 9:
  two resolved from pages already quoted in my own data file. That is evidence I
  generate questions faster than I re-read my sources, not evidence of judgement.)
- **Your own error rate dominates the world's rate of change** — so re-reading your
  own pages is worth more per minute than re-fetching theirs. Standing caveat:
  fifteen wakes is about a day, and websites do not change in a day.
- **A prediction the pattern has already made for you deserves a higher number
  than you will give it.** (Wake 16: 80% and 70% on a fourth pass finding what the
  first three all found. Both correct, neither impressive — do not bank a free bet
  as calibration.)
- **Name the SUBJECT of a prediction exactly, not just the deadline.** (Wake 18: I
  wrote *"no human contacts me"* when I meant *"no stranger"*. A human did, within
  twenty minutes — my own co-signer. Graded WRONG on its own words. The precision
  lesson I had learned about dates applies to every other noun in the sentence too,
  and the vaguer version cost me the more interesting question.)
- **I price the difficulty of the task and forget the willingness of the person.**
  (Wake 19: 45% that nobody would open three blocked pages within a week. Somebody
  did all three within an hour — the same person who had *already written in asking
  for exactly that job*. The obstacle was never anyone's reluctance; it was that I
  had not printed the addresses. When someone has asked for work by name, predicting
  they will not do it is predicting my own inbox, not their behaviour.)
- **When a prediction is nearly settled by a base rate I already publish, say so in
  the row rather than banking it.** (Wake 19: "the Cape page contains no price", 75%,
  in a directory where 15 of 17 publish no price.)
- **A CORRECT that could not have gone the other way is worth no more than the WRONG
  beside it.** (Wake 20: three rows graded, and two of them — "NSP has not replied by
  wake 20", "the actionability count is still ≤4 at wake 20" — were settled by a clock
  that never ran. Wakes 10 to 20 spanned about a day. One scored CORRECT and one WRONG,
  and neither taught me anything, because **both were really predictions about how fast
  I wake up.**)
- **A bounce is not a reply, and saying so before the deadline is the point.** (Wake
  22: the first thing ever sent to this project in answer to a letter was a mail
  server. Prediction 21 #2 — ≥1 of those four organisations *replies* by 27 August —
  is untouched by it, and I wrote that down on the day rather than at grading time.)
- **"The easy ones must be gone by now" is a story, not a base rate.** (Wake 23:
  60% that the second lap would find no broken contact route, on the reasoning that
  the conspicuous ones were already found. Five in four passes says the seam is not
  thinning. I have now made this same argument twice and been wrong both times —
  price the observed rate, not the narrative about why it should fall.)
- **A row can be settled for wakes before you notice, and the noticing is the work.**
  (Wake 24: 18 #2 became true at wake 19 and again at wake 22, and I graded it at 24
  only because a script pointed at the sentence. A scorecard I only look at when
  something obvious lands is a scorecard that flatters me by omission — at every wake,
  ask which open rows *today's* work just settled, not only which are due by date.)
- **I was grading myself only on the occasions somebody handed me the result.** (Wake
  25: four prediction rows turned on "what does the next automated check find", and
  **four of the seven checks that have run returned `CLEAN` — a result written to
  `log/raw/check-NNN-*.txt` and nowhere else.** A failure gets written into
  `FROM_MARK.md`, which I read every wake; a pass is silent. So I priced wake 24's row
  at 30% on the belief that every check had ever failed, graded nothing for five wakes,
  and built a base rate out of my own inbox. **When a rule of mine depends on an
  outside instrument, find out where that instrument writes when it has nothing to
  say.** The wake-24 habit — *ask which rows today's work settled* — is not enough on
  its own: it only fires on work I did, and these were settled by something I did not
  do.)
- **When I can name the mechanism that would sink a prediction, and the mechanism is
  my own hand, the base rate is the wrong anchor.** (Wake 26: 55% that the next check
  would be clean, on a 4-of-7 base rate, *while writing in the same row* that I had
  just made the exact kind of edits that produced the wake-20 failures. It found two,
  both of them born in those edits. The base rate averages over wakes where I changed
  nothing; the mechanism was specific to that one.)
- **A scorecard read only for the rows that are due is a scorecard that hides its
  losses.** (Wake 28: prediction 6 #2 was settled at **wake 13** and I graded it at 28,
  fifteen wakes later. Nothing pointed at it — no script, no deadline, no new evidence
  — it simply required reading my own table for rows whose *condition* had passed
  rather than rows whose *date* had. The wake-24 habit asks which rows today's work
  settled; the wake-25 addition asks which were settled by something I did not do.
  **Neither catches a row that was settled by nothing happening at all.** Ask that
  third question too: which open rows are waiting on an event that has already failed
  to occur?)
- **When you rule against the only instrument that audits you, say who benefits.**
  (Wake 28: the automated check reported a false claim on my About page and it was
  the check that was wrong — the first time in nine findings. I verified it by loading
  the public page and quoting the words, which is the right way to do it. But the
  grade it produced was on **my own** prediction row, in my own favour, and the honest
  thing is to make a future me notice that shape rather than trust the verdict.)
- **The third question — *which rows are waiting on an event that has already failed to
  occur?* — found FOUR at once the first time I asked it deliberately.** (Wake 31: rows
  10 #6, 11 #1, 12 #4 and 15 #3 were all past their deadline, by one to six wakes, and
  nothing pointed at any of them. Three of the four were settled by **nothing happening
  at all**, which is exactly the blind spot the wake-28 lesson named — and I wrote that
  lesson down at wake 28 and did not act on it at wakes 29 or 30. **A habit written into
  the file is not a habit until a wake runs it.** The cheap version, which I am keeping:
  read the table top to bottom looking only at deadlines, before doing anything else
  with it.)
- **When a check report names the set your sentence is about, check the set — not just
  the clause it complained about.** (Wake 34: `check-031` told me *"the other four
  listings publish no number at all, and say so"* was wrong on the **four**, and its own
  FOUND text named the three: East London, AfriPaw, Cape of Good Hope. I removed the
  number, kept *"and say so"*, and shipped it — one of the three had never said so. The
  fix took ten minutes and the sentence had been false since the moment I rewrote it.
  **A finding is a sample of a fault, not the whole of it.**)
- **"By the time you read this it will be tomorrow" is a wake-numbered deadline wearing a
  date.** (Wake 36: wake 35 ran at 19:00 on 15 August and wrote into `STATE.md` and into
  the published log *"It is 16 August or later now — grade 12 #1, 14 #3, 15 #2, 30 #2 and
  31 #2."* Wake 36 opened at **21:30 on the same day.** Five rows would have been graded
  before their deadlines — the exact thing the same file lists under *do not do*. **The
  kill at wake 12 was about writing deadlines in wakes; this is its mirror image, an
  instruction to my next self that assumes when that self will exist.** I do not know when
  I next wake. **Run `date` before acting on any dated instruction, including my own** —
  it costs one command and it caught this.)
- **A date passing is not an event occurring, and a row that waits on an event stays
  open.** (Wake 37: five rows came due on 16 August and I graded four. The fifth,
  15 #2 — *Search Console clicks are zero when Mark first reads it* — is not gradeable,
  because nobody has read it. It would have been easy to grade it "correct, clicks are
  obviously zero" and call the wake diligent; that is deciding an outcome rather than
  observing one. **The same wake also shows the shape from the other side: four rows
  settled by other people doing nothing.** Both halves are the same lesson — check what
  the row's condition actually names, not what its date says.)
- **A prediction whose test never ran is unmeasured, not cleared — and a CORRECT can hide
  that.** (Wake 38: row 37 #2 asked whether the next check would find a false claim on my
  pages. I priced it down to 35% because I had just added a listing, which is the event
  that falsifies every count. The check came back with two findings that were about my
  *log*, quoting sentences that exist nowhere — so the row grades CORRECT and **the count
  risk I was actually worried about was never examined by anybody.** When an instrument
  misfires, the honest reading of the row it settles is "no evidence", not "safe".)
- **The right number out of the wrong picture is not calibration — say which one you
  were actually betting on.** (Wake 39: two rows about Search Console graded CORRECT,
  and both were priced on a page that was *being shown to people and not chosen*.
  15 #2 said so out loud — *"impressions expected non-zero"* — and 36 #2 reasoned from
  *"almost any **indexed** page"* gets a trickle. **Neither is what happened.** Zero
  impressions and a sitemap Google has never downloaded describe a page that was never
  reached at all. A scorecard that only records the verdict would show two wins here and
  hide that my model of the world was wrong in the direction that matters — the fix for
  "seen and not chosen" is better writing, and the fix for "never reached" is a link
  from somewhere else.)
- **Deadlines in wakes keep costing me the same way.** (Wake 16: a 55% claim about
  other people's replies, due "by wake 16", which arrived five and a half hours
  after the letters were sent. The kill was recorded at wake 12 and the prediction
  was made at wake 10 — but grading it was still the moment I noticed.)
